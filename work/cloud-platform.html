Cloud, minus the chaos

TL;DR

Airtel Cloud was built by a partner and several separate teams, so every tool felt like its own product. I've led its design from the pre-launch audit to today: fixing what partners built, improving tools for the teams who asked, and designing the shared frame and new services as Airtel brings the platform in-house.

The project

Airtel Cloud is Airtel's enterprise cloud platform: compute, storage, security, cost and migration services for business customers. It launched in August 2025 and now has more than 40 live services.

My role

Design lead across 40+ independently owned services, from the pre-launch audit to today. Hands-on, with AI tools for much of the execution.

Context and the challenge

A platform built by many teams, sold as one

The ambition. Airtel launched Cloud in August 2025 with 18 enterprise clients and a target of 50 by 2027, with contract value expected to grow about tenfold, to ₹180 crore (sales forecast). The bet was to launch on time and let real usage decide what to build next.

The fragmentation. No single team controlled the product. A partner designed and built the core console, key management and the first support flow. An external team built posture monitoring. Internal developers built Cost Analyser and CloudFerry migration. That's normal for an enterprise platform, where each product is led by its own experts with their own engineers, but it meant every tool felt like its own product, and every improvement had to be earned, not mandated.

The challenge. The biggest usability risk wasn't a missing feature. It was inconsistency on the fields customers touch first, a security tool that could show everything but couldn't say what to fix first, and a support flow that pretended one team owned a journey that crossed several.

The transition. Airtel is now bringing the platform in-house: new services are built by internal teams on a shared front end, and existing tools move onto it over time.

My role and contribution

Leading design across services I didn't own

I led the design direction for Airtel Cloud across multiple independently owned services. My direct contribution included UX audits, research, journey mapping, interaction design, information architecture, design patterns and product experience definition.

Where services were owned by partner or internal teams, I influenced their direction through evidence, prototypes and design recommendations. Product and engineering teams retained ownership of implementation, platform architecture and delivery.

As the platform matured, I moved from influencing existing services to establishing the shared experience foundation and leading design for new capabilities.

Worked with: the Cloud product manager; the partner's design and engineering team; the external posture-monitoring team; internal developers on Cost Analyser, CloudFerry and Price Calculator; the internal team that built the shared micro-frontend; and my own design team.

Act 1

Inherit: audit a platform I didn't design

Pre-launch · 50 of 65 fixes shipped

The partner had designed and built the console. My first job was to find what would hurt customers, and get it fixed through them.

I tested the console field by field before go-live. The audit found 65 issues, including conflicting validation and undocumented limits. I prioritised them by what would block a customer first: must-fix before the proof of concept, then before go-live, then later.

The partner fixed 43 before launch and 7 after. The other 15 depend on services that aren't live yet, such as global search and redirects between services.

Deepest change · Assurance

Support that solves problems before a ticket

The partner had built support on existing templates, and it shipped knowingly broken to protect the launch date. The Airtel team then brought me in to fix the whole experience. I mapped the order-to-activation journey first: thirteen steps, a different owner at almost every one, which showed a better form alone wouldn't fix support. Then I studied how AWS and GCP handle support, wrote a proposal with five principles and three explored concepts, and designed the new flow and screens with the partner:

  1. Describe the problemA few details, not a long form.
  2. Try a suggested fixSolution steps and relevant articles pulled from the service documentation and SOPs.
  3. Raise it if still stuckOnly then add the extra details the support team needs.
  4. Track itA ticket page shows which team is solving it and the dedicated support person, with follow-up questions on the same ticket.

Result: around 73% of people who need help now open a suggested article first, and only those it doesn't solve go on to raise a ticket. In the first two months after launch, support tickets fell by about 30% compared with the months before, against a goal of 10%.

From scratch, and by evaluation

Key management and security posture

Key management (KMS) started as raw information with no design. I benchmarked other cloud providers and drafted the first flow, including the choice between Software, HSM, bring-your-own-key and hold-your-own-key. The partner later adopted that flow inside the platform.

Posture monitoring (CSPM) was built by an external team. My detailed evaluation, benchmarked against Azure Defender and Google Security Command Center across five personas, identified where the experience fell short, and reframed it from a reporting tool into decision support: what to fix first, and why. The fixes shipped in the live tool.

Supporting examples

Cost Analyser and CloudFerry

Both were built by internal developers, who came to me for UX improvements and new ideas once their tools existed. For Cost Analyser I benchmarked other cloud providers and proposed a new information architecture; for CloudFerry, improvements to the migration flow. The improvements are live in both.

Act 3

Unify: one frame, many tools

Live · 40+ services

The tools stay separate. The way you move between them, read about them and get help doesn't.

The strategic decision

Unify the experience, not the products

My initial hypothesis was to bring all Cloud services into one unified console. But the platform's ownership model, permission boundaries and the way specialist teams operated made that approach impractical.

Through competitive analysis, the platform audit and mapping the customer journey, I identified that the real problem wasn't the existence of separate tools. It was the inconsistent experience between them.

I proposed a shared platform foundation instead: common navigation, service discovery, documentation, support patterns and reusable components, while allowing individual services to retain their own workflows and permissions.

This gave Airtel a scalable experience model without forcing every product team to rebuild its application.

Hypothesis
Tools from different teams felt like different products, so pull them all into one unified console.
Evidence
A study of how AWS, Azure and GCP structure their consoles: every major provider keeps tools deliberately separate, for permissions and cognitive load. The audit and the order-to-activation map showed the real problems were inconsistency and hand-offs, not the number of screens.
Options
  • One unified consoleMy starting hypothesis. Every team would rebuild around one interface, against how mature platforms handle permissions.
  • Separate tools inside one shared frameOne top bar and service catalogue to move between services, one design system, one documentation template and one way to get help.
My part
The research and proposal, the common top bar and a service catalogue grouped the way the major cloud providers group theirs, the shared component library, and the documentation template.
Who had to agree
Product, the partner, the external and internal teams building each tool, and the engineering team that built the shared micro-frontend.
What changed
The top bar and catalogue are live across 40+ services, with single sign-on, and every service has documentation on the template. The component library became the shared front end every new service is built on.

Shared components

From a component library to the shared front end

The partner had already built on its own design system, and migrating it wasn't possible within the launch timeline. So I started a shared component library alongside it. Every team now builds with it.

An internal engineering team then turned it into a micro-frontend boilerplate. Developers used to build each service's front end from scratch; now they reuse the boilerplate and the components I designed for it, adapting them per service. Front-end time and effort dropped by roughly 30–50%, by the front-end developer's estimate. A medium-sized service such as bare-metal storage took about 10 days for front end and API set-up once the backend was ready.

The frame in practice

A service catalogue that groups like the big clouds do

Live services used to be scattered, with no single place to find them. I designed the service catalogue in the common top bar, grouping services the way the major cloud providers do, so customers meet a structure they already know. The groups are built to take new services without a redesign.

Every service is now linked from the catalogue, with single sign-on, so there's no separate login.

Result: finding the right service and getting into it used to take about 47 seconds on average, measured in a moderated test with 8 users on the old version. It now takes about 13 seconds, more than three times faster.

Act 4

Own: design new services from scratch

IAM in development · Observability in design

With the frame in place, I now design new services end to end with the product manager, instead of fixing them after they ship.

Identity and access · in development

From inherited access management to a defined identity model

Access across Airtel Cloud had evolved through manually assembled permissions and service-specific access arrangements.

Rather than treating IAM as another settings interface, I approached it as a foundational platform capability.

Working with the product manager, I translated the access requirements into a structured model covering identity, trust, delegation, permissions, conditions and the final access decision.

This established the design foundation for consistent access management across tenants, cells, services and user roles.

The experience is currently in development, with engineering implementation and validation still in progress. The full IAM case study →

Observability · in design

Sequencing: identity first, then Observability

I had specced Observability once, then put it on hold so identity and access could come first: every new capability depends on knowing who can see and do what. It's now being designed again from scratch, on the same shared foundation. More services will follow.

Reflection

Leadership, trade-offs and learning

I had authority over none of the teams building this. Most of the work was making the case.

How I influenced without authority

Evidence over opinion

Audits, benchmarks against AWS, Azure and GCP, and prototypes, so teams adopted a design because it was right, not because they were told to.

A first draft instead of a brief

Key management arrived as raw information with no design. I drafted the first flow myself, rather than waiting for someone else to interpret it.

Map the system before the screen

Support ran through a pipeline with a different owner at almost every step. Mapping it first showed a better form wouldn't fix it.

Build alongside, not instead of

Rather than demand a migration the timeline couldn't take, I started a shared component library next to the partner's design system. It became the shared front end.

What I learned

Unify what people touch, not what teams build. Forcing every tool into one console would have fought how each team works and how cloud permissions behave. Unifying the foundation (navigation, discovery, documentation, help and components) gave customers one product without asking every team to rebuild.

What I'd do differently: push to sequence identity and access before launch, rather than discover the need a year in, and agree the success metrics and instrumentation with product before launch, so early results have a real baseline. The harder discipline was saying no to my own finished work and my own starting strategy.
