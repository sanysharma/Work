The project

Airtel Cloud is Airtel's enterprise cloud console: compute, storage, security, cost and migration tools for business customers. It launched in August 2025 on a deliberately minimal scope.

My role

Design lead, from pre-launch audit through launch to the design handover in April–May 2026. I owned the research and design decisions, and used AI tools for much of the execution.

Why the Cloud platform?

Airtel launched Cloud with 18 enterprise clients and a target of 50 by 2027, with contract value expected to grow about tenfold, to ₹180 crore (sales forecast). The bet was to launch on time and let real usage decide what to build next. By October 2026 it had 35 clients.

No single team controlled the product. An external agency built the core console, a security vendor owned key management with a spec and no design, a partner team owned security posture (CSPM), and two internal teams built Cost Analyser and CloudFerry migration on their own. That is normal for an enterprise platform: each product is led by its own domain experts with their own engineers. It still works that way; another team is now building IAM and the micro-frontend shell, and will take on Observability next. So every improvement had to be earned, not mandated.

The problem: a platform that couldn't explain itself

The biggest usability risk wasn't a missing feature. It was inconsistency on the fields customers touch first, a security tool that could show everything but couldn't say what to fix first, and a support flow that pretended one team owned a journey that crossed five.

I started this project sure that everything should be unified. Research proved me wrong.

The decision that shaped everything

A shared design language, not one console

What triggered it
Six tools built by five teams felt like six products. My first instinct was to pull them into one unified experience.
Evidence
A study of how AWS, Azure and GCP structure their consoles: every major provider keeps tools deliberately separate, for permissions and cognitive load. The pre-launch audit (65 issues) and the order-to-activation map (13 steps, a different owner at almost every one) showed the real problems were inconsistency and hand-offs, not the number of screens.
Options
  • One unified consoleMy starting hypothesis. It needed every team to rebuild around one interface, against how mature platforms handle permissions.
  • A shared design language across separate toolsCommon components, one documentation template and one way to get help, adopted by each team in its own build.
My part
The research, the written proposal, the component library and documentation template, and prototypes each team could build to.
Who had to agree
Product, and each of the five delivery teams, none of which reported to me: the external agency, the security vendor, the partner team and two internal teams.
What changed
Every team builds with the shared component library, and engineering turned it into a micro-frontend boilerplate for new services. Every service uses the documentation template. The vendor built KMS to my prototype.

What the research showed

Inconsistency and hand-offs, not missing features

The decision in practice · 1

Support that solves problems before a ticket

Assurance, the support layer, shipped knowingly broken to protect the launch date. Six to eight months later I rebuilt it from a written proposal with five principles and three explored concepts. The new flow replaced an out-of-the-box ServiceNow case form:

  1. Describe the problemA few details, not a long form.
  2. Try a suggested fixSolution steps and relevant articles pulled from the service documentation and SOPs.
  3. Raise it if still stuckOnly then add the extra details the support team needs.
  4. Track itA ticket page shows which team is solving it and the dedicated support person, with follow-up questions on the same ticket.

Result: since launch, more than 30% of cases are solved without a ticket, and support tickets are down by about 30%, against a goal of 10%.

The decision in practice · 2

Key management from a spec, and security that prioritises

The key management service (KMS) came from the vendor as a technical spec with no design. I designed the whole interaction model — including the Software, HSM, bring-your-own-key and hold-your-own-key choice — and the agency built the live integration to my prototype.

For CSPM, I benchmarked the product against Azure Defender and Google Security Command Center across five personas, from security teams to executives. That reframed it from a reporting tool into decision support: what to fix first, and why.

Research & testing

Field-level testing before go-live found 65 issues, including conflicting validation and undocumented limits. 43 were fixed before launch and 7 after. The other 15 depend on services that aren't live yet, such as global search, unified navigation and redirects between services.

Bottlenecks

Influence without authority

Most of what slowed this down sat outside design. What got in the way, and how I worked around it:

No authority over any of the five teams building the product

I led with evidence — audits, benchmarks and prototypes — so teams adopted the design because it was right, not because they were told to.

The agency had already built on its own design system

Migrating it wasn't possible within the go-to-market timeline, so I started a shared component library. Every team now builds with it, and engineering turned it into a micro-frontend boilerplate so new services go live faster.

Key management arrived as a technical spec with no design

I designed the whole interaction model from the spec, rather than waiting for someone else to interpret it.

Support ran through a pipeline with a different owner at almost every step

Mapping the lifecycle first showed a better form wouldn't fix it; it needed a cross-system journey.

Impact metrics weren't instrumented at launch

Early figures come from support data and task-success checks after launch. Console task success (~70%) is still below the 85% goal.

What I learned

The identity lesson

A year in, the deepest finding surfaced: access permissions were still being assembled by hand, which made identity the platform's real constraint. I shelved a finished Observability spec to work on it, and it became the next case study.

What I'd do differently: push to sequence identity and access before launch, rather than discover the need a year in. The harder discipline here was saying no to my own finished work and my own starting strategy.
