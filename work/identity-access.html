00My role & ground

I've led IAM design since June 2026, as a distinct engagement after handing over the wider Cloud Platform scope. The Product Manager had begun documenting requirements; from there I built the model, the information architecture and the working prototype, and I'm still leading it as dependencies move in parallel.

There was no PRD when I started, and engineering had already committed to modelling closely on AWS IAM, which shaped my earliest work directly.

01Situation

The external partner building Airtel's Cloud Management Platform had no IAM module, and that gap was costing the business. Prospective clients asked for IAM in sales pitches; without it, deals couldn't proceed.

Leadership made two decisions at once: build IAM as the priority, and build it in-house for control and speed. IAM became the first system Airtel brought back internally, not because the partner's work was bad, but because depending on a partner for identity and access was itself the risk.

02Stakes

Deals were stalling in-pitch specifically over IAM's absence, a direct commercial cost. The committed target is January 2027. This is a large, interdependent build, not a scoped feature.

03Complexity

IAM has to reconcile more concepts than most modules ever will:

IdentitiesGroupsAccounts & CellsRolesPermission setsPoliciesWorkloadsService identitiesIdentity providersResource accessTrustDelegationConditionsGovernance

The challenge was never any one of these. It was building one coherent mental model across all of them, from a standing start, while a legacy access model was the only thing customers had.

04Research & discovery

Three research threads ran in parallel, each answering a different question.

What does the closest reference actually do?

A screen-by-screen audit of AWS IAM (Permission Sets, Policies, Roles and their creation flows), since engineering had committed to an AWS-adjacent approach and there was no PRD to work from instead.

What does good multi-cloud practice look like?

Airtel's own infrastructure runs on both GCP and AWS, so an AWS-only study wasn't enough. I sought out a multi-cloud IAM practitioner on another internal team, who pushed the thinking further: a provider-agnostic identity model, a unified cloud-connector strategy, org-level guardrails, and just-in-time privileged access as a roadmap capability.

What's the baseline we're replacing?

The colleague who manages today's access model walked me through how fragmented it is: identity split across three systems, each needing synced copies of the same user-to-tenant mapping, with Super Admin sitting outside Cell-based scoping as a structural shortcut the new model couldn't inherit.

A fourth input was deliberately not adopted. The external partner was building its own IAM and gave a walkthrough. I mined it for good ideas without intending to adopt it; staying independent was the point.

What I uncovered
  • The legacy model's core flaw wasn't a missing feature. Super Admin worked outside the Cell-scoping model entirely, an inconsistency baked into the system IAM had to replace.
  • AWS has no separate module for Conditions; they're set inline. That contradicted what Product wanted and became a real disagreement (see Key decisions).
  • Coupling identity too tightly to AWS-specific constructs would be a mistake Airtel's multi-cloud reality couldn't afford.

05My intervention

Rather than starting from screens, I started from definitions, separating concepts that get conflated everywhere else: Permission vs. Role vs. Permission Set vs. Policy, and later Trust vs. Delegation.

The core model became the spine of the whole module:

01ProvisionSuper Admin creates a Tenant Admin and dedicates a Cell
02DefineRoles, permission sets and conditions describe what's allowed
03AssignUsers and groups get roles, scoped to Cells
04AssumeA user takes on a role to act, and every decision is traceable

No Cell means no console access, regardless of role. Once a Tenant Admin has access, they can create Cells and users themselves. The Account → Tenant → Cell hierarchy, with Cell as the access gate, is a genuine structural divergence from both AWS and GCP; neither has a direct equivalent.

06Key decisions

  • Conditions: a real disagreement, worked through openly. I put five clarifying questions to the Product Manager, including why Conditions needed their own module when AWS handles them inline. His answer was cognitive load, since one condition can apply to many roles, and he was open to authoring them inline too. The same thread settled that policies stay bundled into permission sets, there are no customer inline policies, and resource policies are managed separately.
  • Split Trust from Delegation. The first design treated them as one module, weighted toward Trust. When Product flagged that Delegation was being treated as a variant, I reworked it into two genuinely separate flows.
  • One Overview page, not two. I first built separate Admin and Cloud User experiences. Review pushed toward one page, and the answer was a single page trimmed by persona.
  • Corrected myself before anyone else did. I proposed a tree to show a user's Accounts & Cells, then realised a tree breaks down when search must show matched accounts and cells together, and moved to an expandable-row list.
  • Adopted "Decision Trace" as explainability language, on my own terms. An AI review surfaced the framing after the fact; I changed its sequence before adopting it.

07From UX fixes to platform thinking

Screens

Email as the primary identifier, "Add User" over "Create User", listing columns reordered.

Flows

Full-page user creation; Trust and Delegation as real flows; a login flow with no redirect for non-root users and support for multiple sign-in providers.

Platform

Provision → Define → Assign → Assume and Account → Tenant → Cell applied across every domain, with Decision Trace as a cross-cutting explainability layer.

Ecosystem

IAM as the mechanism by which Airtel takes control back from an external partner, one system at a time.

10What this taught me

The job isn't avoiding AI-assisted thinking. It's knowing precisely which parts of the output are actually yours.

When an AI handed back a clean "Decision Trace" framing after the fact, the easy paths were to reject it as not mine or adopt it wholesale. Neither was honest. Changing its sequence before adopting it was the real test of whether I understood the model well enough to improve on someone else's articulation of it.

The second lesson was about role change without loss of ownership. Once my hands-on production scope shrank, most of the leverage came from review, decisions and AI-directed execution: the Conditions synthesis, the Trust/Delegation split, the tree-to-list call. None of it required drawing every pixel.
